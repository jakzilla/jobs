US Job Market Visualizer

A research experiment in AI-assisted analysis

The Original

Andrej Karpathy—former head of AI at Tesla, founding member of OpenAI—published an open-source project analysing AI's impact on 342 US occupations from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. He used Claude as a research assistant to score each occupation's exposure to AI on a single 0–10 axis, creating an interactive treemap where rectangle size represents employment and colour represents AI exposure.

The entire project was built with AI assistance. Karpathy created a single prompt.md file containing all BLS data and had Claude analyse it. It was a snapshot: one score per occupation, one moment in time, one axis of analysis.

View Karpathy's original project

This Fork

This fork extends Karpathy's single-axis model into a multi-dimensional, time-varying analysis. Where the original asked "which jobs are exposed to AI?", this fork asks "which jobs will actually be disrupted, when, and what are the downstream consequences?"

Why the original model needed extending

Karpathy's model has an explicit design assumption: "if the job can be done entirely from a home office on a computer, AI exposure is inherently high. Conversely, jobs requiring physical presence, manual skill, or real-time human interaction have a natural barrier." Physical work is treated as a shield against AI. Roofers score 0, janitors score 1, construction laborers score 0. In Karpathy's framework, these occupations are effectively safe. But the model doesn't score low for physical jobs because it assessed the physical AI threat and found it small—it scores low because physical AI is entirely absent from the model. There is no robotics axis. The only question asked is "how much of this work is digital?"

Karpathy published his project on March 14, 2026. Even in the week since, the case for a separate physical AI axis has only strengthened. Tesla went from a person in a robot costume (2022) to converting Fremont production lines to manufacture Optimus humanoid robots (2026) in under four years. They intend to produce millions of units per year. Boston Dynamics, Figure, Agility, and others are on similar trajectories. The moment you ask "what about robots?", those tens of millions of low-scoring physical jobs face a completely separate threat vector that Karpathy's model has nothing to say about. Warehouse workers, truck drivers, agricultural labourers, factory assemblers, janitors, delivery drivers, cleaners—they score near zero on digital AI exposure, but they represent 30+ million jobs that face a distinct disruption.

The physical AI timeline in this model uses the smartphone adoption curve as an analogy. If 2026 is the iPhone 1 moment for humanoid robots, then by 2036 we reach the iPhone X equivalent. By 2017, smartphones had created entirely new industries (ride-hailing, food delivery, mobile payments), destroyed others (point-and-shoot cameras, paper maps, DVD rental), reached over 2 billion units worldwide, and dropped from luxury pricing to $200–300 for a capable device. Apply the same trajectory: by 2036, producers like Tesla will be manufacturing millions of humanoid robots per year at commodity pricing ($15–25K), deployed across warehousing, agriculture, food service, cleaning, delivery, security, and manufacturing.

A common mistake—one this model's own earlier iterations made—is to assume physical AI develops linearly. It doesn't. The manufacturing scales with production lines, but the intelligence behind the robots scales exponentially, because embodied AI training is increasingly done in simulation. Platforms like Google's Genie, NVIDIA Isaac, and similar tools allow robots to practise tasks millions of times in virtual environments before ever touching the physical world—effectively giving them an imagination. This means embodied AI can leverage the same exponential compute scaling as digital AI for learning, while physical deployment scales with manufacturing capacity. The result is that each generation of robot is dramatically more capable than the last, not incrementally better.

By splitting the score into digital and physical axes, this fork tracks both waves independently. Digital AI moves first and faster. Physical AI follows on a trajectory that is slower to start but accelerating rapidly. The two waves converge by the mid-2030s, creating a period where both knowledge work and physical work face simultaneous disruption.

The second limitation is temporal. A single snapshot treats the 2026 job market as a fixed state. But the most important policy question isn't which jobs are exposed—it's when. An occupation with high exposure but massive regulatory friction (e.g. physicians) has a fundamentally different trajectory from one with high exposure and zero barriers (e.g. data entry clerks). And two occupations with identical automation potential can have opposite employment outcomes depending on whether demand is elastic—cheaper legal services might mean vastly more legal work—or fixed—there are only so many insurance claims to process.

These distinctions matter because they change the policy response. A problem arriving in 2028 for clerical workers requires different interventions than one arriving in 2036 for construction trades.

What changed

  • 4 scoring axes instead of 1: digital AI exposure (screen-based, automatable work), physical/embodied AI exposure (susceptibility to robotics), adoption friction (regulatory, social, and institutional barriers), and demand elasticity (does cheaper work create more demand, or is demand fixed?)
  • 3 time horizons (2028, 2031, 2036) instead of a single snapshot, allowing viewers to see how disruption evolves over time
  • Composite impact score that weighs all four axes to estimate net employment impact
  • Narrative analysis for every metric and horizon, grounded in specific data points from the dataset
  • Global impact page analysing how US company automation decisions cascade into economies that depend on American outsourcing—Philippines BPO, Indian IT services, Bangladeshi garments, and more
  • Diffusion-calibrated scoring using actual AI adoption data from March 2026 (McKinsey enterprise surveys, GitHub Copilot adoption statistics, BPO industry revenue data), rather than assuming a simple technology lag

How This Was Built

The human behind this fork has zero coding experience—no programming background, no data science training, no web development skills. The entire project was built in a single extended Claude Code session through natural language direction.

The human's role was setting direction, asking questions, challenging assumptions, and requesting recalibration. All the judgment calls—such as "your physical AI scores are too high for 2031" and "you're carving out artificial human niches that humanoid robots would handle"—came from the human. None of the implementation did.

The AI agents' role was execution. Claude Code orchestrated multiple parallel sub-agents to handle different workstreams simultaneously:

  • 8 scoring agents ran in parallel, each scoring all 342 occupations against a different prompt (digital exposure across 3 horizons, physical exposure across 3 horizons, adoption friction, demand elasticity)—producing 2,736 individual scores
  • Research agents gathered real-world data: current AI adoption rates from McKinsey, Gartner, and Stack Overflow surveys; BPO industry statistics; country-level outsourcing dependency data; technology diffusion models
  • Frontend agents built the interactive treemap, stats dashboard, analysis panels, and design system
  • Analysis agents wrote narrative commentary grounded in actual computed statistics from the dataset

Multiple rounds of rescoring were done when the human identified calibration issues—overestimating physical AI timelines, underestimating digital AI adoption speed, incorrectly assuming high salaries provide insulation from automation.

No code was written by the human at any point. The human's contribution was entirely editorial: direction, criticism, judgment, and taste.

What This Would Have Required Without AI

An honest estimate of the traditional resources needed to produce this project:

  • Research & Data Analysis — Scoring 342 occupations across 8 dimensions requires reading each occupation's BLS description, understanding its task composition, and making a calibrated judgment. At roughly 15 minutes per score, 2,736 scores equals approximately 680 hours of analyst time. A team of 2–3 research analysts working full-time for 4–6 weeks.
  • Web Development — Building an interactive treemap with canvas rendering, responsive design, multiple metric views, tooltips, and three-page navigation. An experienced frontend developer: 1–2 weeks.
  • Data Journalism — Writing narrative analysis for each metric and horizon combination (24+ distinct analytical sections), grounded in specific data points. A skilled data journalist: 1–2 weeks.
  • Global Impact Research — Researching outsourcing dependencies for 15+ countries, gathering BPO statistics, garment industry data, and IT services figures. A research analyst: 1 week.
  • Design — UI/UX design for the dashboard, colour system, and responsive layout. A designer: 3–5 days.

Total traditional estimate: A team of 5–6 specialists working for 3–6 weeks, at a cost of $50,000–$150,000 in professional services.

This was done by one person with no technical skills in a single working session, using AI tools that cost less than $100.

This is not a commentary on the quality comparison—a human team might produce more nuanced scoring, better-tested code, and more carefully researched analysis. It is a demonstration of what the leverage ratio looks like when AI handles execution and humans handle judgment.

Methodology & Limitations

Transparency about what this is and what it is not:

  • Scores are generated by an LLM (Claude) analysing BLS occupation descriptions against structured scoring prompts. They are analytical estimates, not empirical measurements.
  • The composite formula—max of digital/physical exposure, modulated by friction and elasticity—is one reasonable approach among many. Different weightings would produce different rankings.
  • Adoption timeline calibration uses real data (McKinsey surveys, GitHub Copilot statistics, BPO industry figures) but extrapolation from 2026 to 2036 is inherently uncertain.
  • The analysis represents one AI system's interpretation of the data. Different analysts—human or AI—would make different judgment calls.
  • The global impact page covers only the US-outsourcing channel. Domestic AI adoption effects in other countries are acknowledged but not quantified.

This is a research tool for exploring scenarios, not a prediction engine.
